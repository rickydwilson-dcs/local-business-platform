# Selling a High-Value Service Without Putting a Price on It

I've written before about [how much a tradesperson website costs](/blog/how-much-does-a-tradesperson-website-cost), and my general position is that transparency wins. Publish a price range, be upfront about what things cost, and you build trust before the phone even rings.

That advice holds for most of the businesses I work with. It doesn't hold for all of them.

## When a Number Does More Harm Than Good

Some services are genuinely bespoke. The price depends on the property, the client, the specifics of the deal, and a number on a page, stripped of that context, doesn't inform anyone. It just sets an expectation that's wrong for most of the people reading it, and a wrong expectation is worse than no expectation at all.

Silvero Homes is a case in point. They're a property development agency working at the premium end of the market, with clients buying or renting high-value homes. There's no sensible headline price for that kind of business. What one property is worth tells you almost nothing about what the next one is worth. Putting a figure on the homepage wouldn't have built trust. It would have undersold the properties at the top of the range and scared off buyers who were never looking at the bottom of it.

The brief was about proving quality through everything else on the page, before a visitor ever asks the question.

## What Has to Do the Work Instead

When price isn't the differentiator, the rest of the site has to earn trust on its own. For Silvero Homes, that started with the brand itself. They already had a strong logo and a clear sense of the market they were serving, so I drew the design direction straight from it: the same colour palette carried consistently through the whole site, rather than a generic template with their logo dropped on top.

Property websites tend to fail in one of two directions: too much competing for attention, or too little for a visitor to act on. For a premium agency, getting that balance right matters more than usual, because the job of the page is to get out of the way of the product. The layout is clean and minimal on purpose: large images, clear headings, and a structure that lets the property photography do the talking rather than surrounding it with copy trying to sell what the photos already show.

The navigation follows the same logic. Available properties, contact details, and just enough about the agency to establish credibility, nothing more. Every element on the page has to earn its place. On a site like this, clutter reads as a business that isn't confident enough to let its own work speak for itself.

## The Details That Build Trust Before a Word Is Read

None of that works if the site is slow. Buyers looking at high-value property are frequently doing it from their phones, on the way to a viewing or checking details while they're already out and about, so the site has to load quickly and hold up on mobile as well as it does on desktop. A beautiful page that stutters on a phone undoes the trust the design just spent so much effort building.

The result was approved on first presentation, which doesn't happen automatically. It's usually a sign the brief was properly understood before a single screen was designed. For a business where the website is often the first impression a prospective buyer gets, that first impression has to hold up to the same standard as the properties themselves.

## If You're Pricing a High-Value Service

For most of the businesses I build [websites](/services/web-design) for, publishing a price is still the right call, for exactly the reasons I laid out in that cost breakdown. But if what you're selling is genuinely bespoke, forcing a number onto the homepage for the sake of transparency can do more damage than leaving it off. In that case, the job shifts. Everything else on the page (the design, the photography, the navigation, the speed) has to do the work a price tag would otherwise have done, and it has to do that work convincingly before anyone gets as far as asking what it costs.
