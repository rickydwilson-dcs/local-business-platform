# What a website redesign should keep

When a business decides to rebuild its website, the instinct is usually to change everything. New design, new copy, new structure, new platform: start again and get it right this time. Sometimes that instinct is correct. A site that's slow, expensive to run, and has no search structure at all doesn't have much worth saving.

But "start again" and "throw everything away" aren't the same decision. Treating them as one is how a redesign destroys value that took years to build. Some things about an existing website are worth more than they look, and a redesign that misses them can leave a business worse off than the one it replaced, even with a better-looking site to show for it.

## When there's genuinely nothing to keep

An Eastbourne plumber's WordPress site is the clearest case of a rebuild that should throw almost everything out. The old site ran on a managed hosting and maintenance package costing £80 a month, and load time was 3.2 seconds, well past what Google tolerates and well past what a visitor searching for an emergency plumber on their phone will wait for. There was a homepage and a contact page. No service pages, no location pages, no structured data. For a trade that lives on local search, a site with that little structure is effectively invisible.

None of that was worth preserving. The platform, the cost, the lack of structure all got replaced. The new site runs on a £25-a-month plan, loads in 0.8 seconds, and gave the business its own content that updates immediately instead of sitting in a support queue.

## What still had to be rebuilt, not just replaced

Throwing out a broken platform isn't the same as throwing out what that platform was trying, badly, to achieve. The old plumber site had no local SEO to speak of, but the business still needed to rank for "plumber Eastbourne" and the towns around it. That visibility didn't exist yet, so the rebuild had to build it rather than simply avoid losing it: 15 dedicated location pages across East Sussex, individual service pages for emergency plumbing, boiler repair, bathroom installation, leak detection and drain unblocking, and LocalBusiness schema markup throughout. By month four, the site ranked on page one of Google for "plumber Eastbourne", a term the business had never appeared for before.

The same logic applies past this one plumber. Local search visibility, and a business's identity as a specific trade serving a specific area, aren't background details a redesign can ignore. If they're missing, they need building. If they already exist, even in limited form, they need migrating with care, not deleting on the assumption that a new site will sort itself out in the rankings eventually.

## What Mad Graphics kept on purpose

Mad Graphics is a different kind of case, because almost nothing about the old site was broken the way the plumber's was. It's a marketing agency, and its own website carries more weight than most: clients judge whether to hire an agency partly by looking at what the agency does for itself. Martin, the founder, needed a site that matched the quality of the agency's actual work: scroll-triggered animations connecting imagery to services, a live Instagram feed that keeps the portfolio current without manual updates, bolder service icons, Google Maps integration for local search.

Nearly everything about the experience changed. What didn't change was the agency's own established colour palette and visual identity. The brand recognition Mad Graphics had already built stayed intact, carried into a more dynamic execution rather than replaced by a generic new one. An agency's brand is one of the few assets it can't afford to lose in its own redesign, because the redesign itself is a demonstration of the agency's judgement. Changing the format while keeping the identity is what let the new site read as a levelled-up version of Mad Graphics rather than a different agency altogether.

## What this means for any redesign

A few principles hold across both projects. These are my own judgement calls, not claims either case study makes outright.

Keep brand identity where it's already carrying weight. If a business's colours, logo, or visual language have built real recognition, a redesign should build on that recognition rather than discard it for the sake of novelty. Good [website design](/services/web-design) sharpens a business's existing identity, using what already works rather than starting from a blank slate.

Migrate search visibility on purpose during a rebuild. Whether a site has years of established rankings or none at all, local SEO structure needs active attention. An existing site's rankings can be lost through careless URL changes. A site with no rankings needs the service and location pages built from scratch, as the plumber's rebuild shows. Either way, it takes deliberate work.

Preserve credibility signals that took time to accumulate. Reviews, portfolio history, and local search presence are the parts of a website that can't be recreated overnight just because the design around them changed. A redesign should carry them forward rather than treat a fresh coat of paint as a reason to start from zero.

Before any redesign, ask what the business has already earned that would be foolish to throw away. Answer that first, and the redesign that follows adds to the business instead of quietly costing it something it can't easily get back.
