# When the website is the pitch: what agencies get wrong on their own site

For most businesses, the website is a description of the work. For a marketing or creative agency, it's a sample of it. A plumber's website doesn't need to prove the plumber is good at plumbing, the boiler either works or it doesn't. An agency's website is the one piece of the agency's own output a prospective client can judge before paying for anything, which raises the stakes considerably. A visitor looking at an agency's homepage isn't just checking opening hours or reading a service list, they're judging, right there, whether this is a business capable of producing something impressive. An ordinary site doesn't just fail to help. It actively works against the pitch before a single email has been sent.

That's the position Mad Graphics was in. Martin, the founder, knew his existing site wasn't doing justice to the work the agency actually produced. The problem wasn't a lack of good projects. It was a website that didn't reflect them.

## The trap most agency sites fall into

Agency websites tend to land in one of two places, and neither is good enough.

The first is the style-led showcase: heavy on visual flourish, built to look impressive in a screenshot, but hard to actually use. A visitor gets an impression of creativity, then struggles to find out what the agency actually does or how to get in touch. It looks the part and loses the enquiry anyway, because the visitor gave up looking for the phone number before they found it.

The second is the plain service-page site: a clear list of what's on offer, easy to navigate, and completely forgettable. It tells a visitor everything except the one thing an agency needs to prove: that it has taste, and that its work stands out from every other agency offering the same list of services in the same layout.

Mad Graphics' original site had fallen into this gap. The challenge for the rebuild wasn't picking one of these two modes. It was landing somewhere that did both: engaging enough to leave an impression, clear enough that a prospective client could understand the offer and get in touch without friction. That's a harder brief than either mode on its own, because most of the obvious ways to add visual impact (bigger imagery, more motion, less on-page text) are also the ways a site becomes harder to read at a glance.

## The real failure mode: a portfolio that goes stale

There's a second problem that's easy to miss because it isn't a design problem at all. An agency's best work is always its most recent work, and a static site goes stale the moment that work stops being updated on the homepage.

This matters more for a creative agency than almost any other kind of business. A tradesperson's site can go months without a content update and nobody notices, the trade itself hasn't changed. An agency's site sitting on the same three projects for a year says something uncomfortable to a prospective client browsing it: is this agency still working? Still good? A portfolio that quietly stops being current can do more damage to an agency's credibility than any single design choice, because it's the kind of thing a visitor absorbs without ever quite registering why the site feels off.

Keeping a portfolio current usually means someone remembering to update it, and that's exactly the kind of task that gets deprioritised the moment a busy studio has actual client work to do. The website ends up last on the list of things the agency has time for, which is exactly backwards for the one asset doing the selling.

## How the rebuild solved both problems at once

The Mad Graphics rebuild used movement to solve the navigability problem. Scroll-triggered animations link imagery directly to the services behind it, so a visitor moving down the page builds an intuitive sense of what Mad Graphics does. The images and the offer read as one thing rather than a gallery sitting next to an unrelated list, so the page stays clear as well as engaging.

Bold, immediately legible service icons do the clarity work that a purely visual showcase usually skips. Each offering is identifiable at a glance, so a visitor gets the basic information at the same time as the visual impression.

The freshness problem got a different kind of fix. Rather than relying on anyone remembering to update the site, the homepage pulls a live Instagram feed straight from Martin's account. Mad Graphics already posts its latest projects there regularly, so the feed updates itself: the homepage always shows recent work without anyone touching the site's content. It also works as a signal in its own right: a visibly active feed tells a visitor the agency is working right now.

Google Maps integration rounds it out with a local anchor, useful for an agency that also needs to show up in local search rather than relying purely on referral and reputation. None of these choices work in isolation. The animation without the icons would still be hard to parse quickly, and the icons without the Instagram feed would still be sitting on last year's work.

## Questions worth asking about your own site

A few quick checks tend to surface which failure mode a site has fallen into.

Can a visitor find your phone number or contact form within a few seconds of landing on the homepage, without scrolling past a full-screen animation first? If not, that's the style-led showcase problem.

Could a competitor swap their logo onto your homepage and nobody would notice the difference? If so, that's the forgettable service-page problem.

When was the newest project on your homepage actually finished? If the answer is longer ago than you'd like to admit, the portfolio has gone stale, and it's costing more credibility than any layout decision would.

## What this means beyond one agency

Scroll animation and Instagram widgets are the implementation. The underlying point is broader: an agency's website carries a heavier burden than most business sites. It has to work as evidence of the work itself, and that evidence has to stay current without depending on someone's discipline to keep it that way.

If your own site is the thing selling the work, it's worth asking which failure mode it's fallen into. All style and no clarity. All clarity and no impression. And whether your portfolio is actually up to date, or just was the last time someone had a spare afternoon.
