# What Makes a Great Website for Scaffolding Companies?

A scaffolding company's website presents a different challenge to most trade websites. You're not just talking to homeowners needing scaffolding for a roof repair. You're also talking to main contractors, developers, property managers, and housebuilders who need a reliable supply chain for ongoing projects. These two audiences have completely different questions and completely different trust requirements.

Getting a scaffolding website right means structuring it to serve both audiences well — without confusing either.

## Understanding Your Two Audiences

**Domestic clients** are homeowners needing scaffolding for a specific project: roof repairs, chimney work, rendering, extensions, loft conversions. They're usually arranging scaffolding because a builder or roofer has told them they need it. Their questions are practical:

- How quickly can you get the scaffold up?
- How long can I keep it?
- How much will it cost roughly?
- Are you insured?

They're often not sure what they need, and they want reassurance that you'll manage it for them.

**Commercial clients** — builders, developers, facilities managers — are more experienced buyers. They've worked with scaffolding companies before. Their questions are different:

- Do you have the right insurance for our site?
- Can you work to our programme and meet deadlines?
- Have you done projects of this scale before?
- Are your operatives CISRS-carded?

A website that only speaks to domestic clients will put off commercial ones. A website that only speaks to commercial clients is unlikely to convert homeowners. The solution is clear navigation that lets each audience find what's relevant to them quickly.

## Key Pages Your Website Needs

**Domestic scaffolding hire.** A dedicated page covering scaffolding for homeowners. Explain the typical use cases (roofing, chimney, rendering, painting, loft conversions), the process of getting a quote, typical lead times for getting scaffolding erected, and roughly how long hire periods work. Remove the uncertainty — most domestic customers have never hired scaffolding before.

**Commercial scaffolding.** A separate page for your commercial offering. Cover the types of projects you handle — new build, refurbishment, facades, industrial — and speak to commercial clients' priorities: programme management, site safety, CITB compliance, working with principal contractors.

**Scaffold inspection certificates.** If you provide independent scaffold inspections and Handover Certificates (SG4:22 compliant), this deserves its own page. It's a specific service that people search for, and it signals your understanding of the industry's safety standards.

**Tube and fitting vs system scaffold.** If you offer both, a brief explanatory page about the difference helps commercial clients specify what they need. Most domestic clients don't know there's a difference — but including it shows technical depth that commercial clients appreciate.

**About / company profile.** Commercial clients in particular want to understand who they're dealing with. How long you've been in business, what areas you cover, approximate capacity, and any notable completed projects.

## Trust Signals That Matter for Commercial Clients

For domestic clients, the most important trust signals are reviews, clear pricing information, and a visible phone number. For commercial clients, the bar is higher.

**Insurance certificates.** Public liability insurance and, for larger projects, contractor's all-risk insurance. Commercial clients will ask for these before engaging you, so you might as well make it easy to see you have them. You don't need to publish the certificates themselves — a statement like "fully insured with £5m public liability" is sufficient on the website.

**CISRS-carded operatives.** The Construction Industry Scaffolding Record Scheme is the industry standard for scaffolder competency. Commercial clients will often ask whether your scaffolders hold CISRS cards. If they do, say so. If you hold CISRS Advanced Scaffolder or Scaffolding Supervisor cards yourself, mention those specifically.

**Health and safety policy.** A brief mention that you operate a formal H&S policy and comply with SG4:22 (the National Access and Scaffolding Confederation's guidance on preventing falls) shows commercial clients that you're serious about site safety.

**Completed projects.** Photos of completed scaffolding on real projects — particularly larger commercial or industrial jobs — demonstrate scale and capability. A portfolio section showing a range of project types is more persuasive to commercial clients than any amount of text.

## Case Study: Colossus Scaffolding

DCS built the website for Colossus Scaffolding, an East Sussex-based scaffolding contractor. Before the website, their business came primarily through word of mouth and personal referrals — their online presence was effectively zero.

The new site was structured around both domestic and commercial audiences, with clear sections for each. Location pages were built for the main towns across their service area. Trust signals — insurance, experience, CISRS-carded team — were prominent throughout.

Within a few months of launch, Colossus were appearing consistently in local search results and receiving enquiries from commercial clients — developers and main contractors — who found them through Google. They've since taken on larger commercial contracts that they simply wouldn't have been considered for without a credible web presence to point people to.

The website didn't just replace word of mouth — it opened a new channel to clients that word of mouth couldn't reach.

## Location Strategy: Think Wider Than Other Trades

Most trades cover a relatively tight local area — an electrician or plumber might rarely travel more than 20 or 25 minutes from their base. Scaffolding is different. The economics of a scaffolding job (lorry, crew, multiple days of hire) mean it's often worth travelling considerably further.

A scaffolding company based in one town might realistically cover a 30 to 50 mile radius for commercial projects, and perhaps 20 to 30 miles for domestic work. This means location pages have a bigger payoff for scaffolding than for most trades.

For a company based in, say, Eastbourne, that might mean location pages targeting Brighton, Lewes, Hastings, Bexhill, Seaford, Newhaven, Hailsham, Uckfield, Tunbridge Wells, and Crowborough — a much wider footprint than a typical trade. Each of those pages picks up local search traffic and tells Google (and potential customers) that you actively cover that area.

## Photos and Portfolio: What Works

Scaffolding is inherently photogenic. Well-built scaffolding on a complex structure — a spire, an industrial building, a multi-storey residential block — is genuinely impressive and shows technical capability.

For domestic clients: photos of scaffolding on typical residential projects, with the house visible and the scaffold looking neat and well-erected. A tidy domestic scaffold is reassuring.

For commercial clients: bigger projects, more complex configurations, ideally with some sense of scale. A scaffold that clearly required engineering experience to design and build is more persuasive than a row of standard tube-and-fit on a terrace.

It's worth investing a small amount of time in decent photography on notable projects. The photos you put on your website will be doing your selling for you long after the job is done.
