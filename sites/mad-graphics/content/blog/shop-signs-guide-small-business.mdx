A well-designed shop sign does more than show your name. It communicates the character of your business, helps people find you, and sets an expectation before a customer even walks through the door. For small businesses in East Sussex — whether you're on a high street in Eastbourne, a market town in Lewes, or a coastal village — getting your signage right is worth taking seriously.

## Types of Shop Signs

Understanding the options helps you have a more productive conversation with your sign maker. The main types used in retail and commercial premises are:

**Fascia signs** are the primary sign above a shop front — the flat panel that carries your business name. These are the most visible sign a shopfront has, and they come in a range of materials: aluminium composite (often called Dibond), acrylic, foam PVC, brushed aluminium, and traditional painted timber. The material choice affects both the look and the longevity.

**Window graphics** use cut or printed vinyl applied directly to the glass. They can range from simple lettering with your hours and contact details to detailed promotional graphics covering a large portion of the window. Window graphics are relatively affordable, easy to update, and can be very effective at communicating what you sell to passing trade.

**A-boards and pavement signs** are freestanding signs placed on the pavement outside your premises. They're excellent for catching the attention of pedestrians who might walk past without looking up, and they're easy to update with daily specials, promotions, or event information. Note that A-boards on public pavements are subject to local authority rules — more on that below.

**Projecting signs** (also called blade signs or hanging signs) extend out from the building face at a right angle. They're particularly useful on streets where pedestrians approach from the side rather than looking straight at the fascia. They're also traditional in character and suit period buildings well.

**Built-up lettering** (individual letters mounted with standoffs so they cast a shadow) gives a premium, three-dimensional look and is often used by businesses wanting to make a stronger impression. Options include flat-cut acrylic, brushed metal, and illuminated letters.

## Planning Permission: What You Need to Know

This is the part that catches many small business owners out. In England, many shop signs are covered by permitted development rights, meaning you don't need planning permission for them — but there are significant exceptions.

**Conservation areas** cover large parts of East Sussex town centres, including much of Lewes, parts of Eastbourne, Rye, and Hastings Old Town. In these areas, the rules are stricter, particularly around illuminated signs. Even signs that would be permitted development elsewhere may require consent in a conservation area.

**Listed buildings** have the tightest restrictions. Any sign attached to a listed building — or, in some cases, within its curtilage — may require listed building consent as well as planning permission. This applies even to window vinyl in some cases. If your premises are listed, always check with your local planning authority before proceeding.

**General rules** for non-illuminated fascia signs in non-designated areas are relatively permissive, but size, projection, and materials can still trigger a requirement for consent. Illuminated signs face more restrictions across the board.

The practical advice: check with East Sussex County Council or your local district council (Eastbourne Borough Council, Lewes District Council, Rother District Council, etc.) before ordering if there's any doubt. A good sign company will be familiar with the local rules and can advise you, but the responsibility for obtaining consent rests with the property owner or occupier.

## Materials: What to Choose and Why

The material choice for a fascia sign is a balance of aesthetics, durability, and budget.

**Aluminium composite panels** (3mm or 4mm Dibond) are the most common substrate for flat fascia signs. They're lightweight, rigid, weather-resistant, and can be printed or vinyl-faced in any colour. They hold up well over years of outdoor exposure and are cost-effective for most applications.

**Acrylic** is popular for its clean, modern look, especially when used for built-up lettering or illuminated signs. It's more brittle than aluminium in large panels but excellent for smaller applications.

**Foam PVC** is a budget option — lighter and cheaper than aluminium composite but less durable outdoors. Suitable for temporary signs or low-exposure applications.

**Timber** remains popular for traditional character, particularly in conservation areas or for businesses where a craft or artisan identity is part of the brand. Properly treated and painted timber can last many years, but it requires more maintenance than synthetic materials.

For window graphics, cast vinyl lasts significantly longer than calendered vinyl — typically 5+ years versus 2–3 years. For permanent window lettering, it's worth paying for the better material.

## Practical Tips for Small Business Owners

**Keep it legible.** The primary job of a fascia sign is to tell people who you are and what you do. A complicated logo treatment that looks great in a design presentation can be hard to read at pavement level. Bold, clear typography almost always outperforms elaborate design at actual sign sizes.

**Think about the whole shopfront.** Your fascia, window graphics, and A-board should work together as a coherent system. Mismatched fonts, colours, and styles across different sign elements look unprofessional and undermine the impression you're trying to create.

**Get a quote that includes installation.** Fitting a fascia sign often involves working at height, drilling into masonry, and dealing with whatever is behind the existing sign. Factor installation into your budget from the start.

**Don't neglect the sides.** If your premises have visible side walls — particularly on a corner plot or near a busy road — they're signage opportunities that many businesses ignore.
