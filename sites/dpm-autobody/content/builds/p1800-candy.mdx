Documented the way an auction house documents one: what it is, what was asked for, how long it
took, who signed it. Every owner gets this, whether or not it ever appears on a website.

We wrote this entry on the day the car went on the transporter: the colour, the time it took, the
hours in it, the list of what the owner asked for, and the size of the photographic file that

## What the owner asked for

Five changes, exactly as the owner listed them. A concours restoration is not always a return to
standard — more often it is a considered argument with the factory, and the argument belongs in
the record.

1. **Candy Red colour** — A translucent colour laid over a metallic base. It reads black in
   shadow and lifts to a deep red where the sun catches it, so the car is never quite the same
   colour twice.
2. **Wire wheel conversion** — Splined hubs in place of the factory bolt pattern, and a chrome
   wire wheel at each corner.
3. **Power steering conversion** — The one change on this list the owner feels rather than sees.
4. **Smoothed chrome bumpers** — Over-riders and their fixings removed, the shells filled,
   levelled and re-plated. Chrome shows every thousandth you leave behind.
5. **Lowered** — Ride height set down from standard, which resets every panel gap and arch line
   you have just spent months getting right.

## Concours evidence

A concours car is judged on its back as well as its face. The underside — floors, exhaust, rear
suspension, pipe runs — was finished to the same standard as the paint, and photographed in full:
the factory identification plate left unpainted, as found, because provenance is not something
you repaint.

## The log

Four entries, each signed by the man who did the work. Nobody writes about a job they did not
stand in front of, and the difficult weeks stay in — a restoration story with no problem in it is
an advertisement.

### Stage 09 — Out in the daylight: "We photographed it more than we painted it"

**Dave — paint and finish**

Over 2,000 photographs went into the file for this one car. Not for the website. For the owner,
and for whoever has it in forty years and wants to know what is underneath the paint before they
cut into it.

People assume the photographs are the marketing and the car is the work. It is the other way
round. The photographs are the only part of the job that survives after the next owner has had it
repainted by somebody else.

I walked round it twice before it went up the ramps onto the transporter. That is not for the
owner's benefit, it is for mine. Once it has gone I cannot do anything about what I have missed.

### Stage 07 — Colour, decided in daylight: "Candy is the one you cannot go back into"

**Paul — colour, mixing, polish**

A candy is a see-through colour over a metallic ground. The depth you are looking at is not
pigment, it is distance — light going down through the colour, off the base, and back out at you.

Which means the colour is built by how much you put on, not by what is in the tin. Two extra
passes on one wing and that wing is a shade darker than the one next to it, for ever. You cannot
blend into a candy and you cannot spot-repair one. If you get it wrong on a door you do the whole
side again.

So it is done in one go, same gun, same pressure, same distance, same speed of arm, every panel,
and nobody talks to you while you are doing it.

### Stage 04 — Metal first: "The bumper came back from the platers with a shadow in it"

**Ellis — metalwork and prep**

Smoothing a bumper is mostly making holes disappear. The over-rider fixings come out, the slots
get filled, and then the shell has to be levelled by hand until there is nothing left to find.

Chrome does not hide work. It magnifies it. Paint sits on a panel and fills a little; plating goes
on in microns and follows every single thing underneath it, and then polishes it up so it catches
the light.

The first one came back from the platers looking right in the workshop and wrong in the doorway.
Held up to daylight there was a soft dark band about four inches long across the face of it, where
I had taken a whisker too much out and left a hollow you could not feel with a thumb. Nothing
measured it. The sky measured it.

Stripped, filled again, levelled with a long file rather than a block this time, and back to the
platers. Three weeks. I would rather find it in the doorway than have the owner find it on a lawn
at a show. It was not a good fortnight and I am not going to write it up as one.

### Stage 05 — Straight before shiny: "Nothing over looked. Nothing left untouched."

**Dave — paint and finish**

That is the line we already write under our own photographs, and it is not a boast, it is a
working rule. It means that if a panel is off the car anyway, it gets done properly, even when
nobody is ever going to look at it.

Thirteen hundred hours is not one heroic month. It is a year of afternoons where the visible
progress is that a gap is now the same at the top as it is at the bottom.

People ask what takes the time. The honest answer is that the last five per cent of a car takes
about a third of it, and there is no way of buying your way past that bit.

## Nine stages

These are our names for them, not the trade's. Durations are the range across the cars in the
shop, not a quotation for yours.

1. **In, and photographed as she came** — Every panel, every gap, every bit of rot, before a
   spanner touches it. *(1 day)*
2. **Off it comes** — Strip to a shell. Bagged, labelled, shelved. Nothing goes in a box unmarked.
   *(1–2 weeks)*
3. **Down to what is really there** — Media blast or chemical strip, then the conversation nobody
   enjoys. *(2–3 weeks)*
4. **Metal first** — Panels made, let in, welded, planished. This is where the time goes.
   *(3 weeks – 5 months)*
5. **Straight before shiny** — Block work over bare metal. If it needs filler to look right, it is
   not right. *(3–6 weeks)*
6. **Primer, and the guide coat** — Etch, epoxy, high-build. Then black mist and a long block,
   twice. *(2–4 weeks)*
7. **Colour, decided in daylight** — Spray-out cards judged outside the booth, against the car's
   own metal. *(2 days)*
8. **Clear, cut and polish** — Coats on, a week to harden, then back to flat by hand and machine.
   *(2–3 weeks)*
9. **Out in the daylight** — Built back up, gaps set, plaque riveted in, then pushed outside and
   walked round twice. *(2–4 weeks)*

## The paint on this car

A specification for one car, not a chart of swatches. Candy is the reason this car photographs
the way it does, and the reason it could not be repaired if anybody ever kerbs it.

Black in shadow, deep red in sun — that is the whole of what a candy does, and it is why the car
looks like two different colours from one photograph to the next.

- **Colour:** Candy Red — DPM's name for it, first item on the owner's list
- **System:** Candy: a translucent colour laid over a metallic ground
- **Where the depth comes from:** Not pigment. Light travelling down through the colour, off the
  base, and back out
- **How the colour is set:** By the number of passes, not by what is in the tin
- **Repairable:** No. A candy cannot be blended into, and cannot be spot-repaired
