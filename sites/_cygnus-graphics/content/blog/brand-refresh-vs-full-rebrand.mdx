## The Brief

An East Sussex trade business — established for fifteen years, with a solid local reputation, a fleet of four vehicles, and an established client base — came to DCS Graphics with a problem they found difficult to articulate: "Something about our brand looks old and we're not sure if we should change it."

They had three vehicles with existing livery, a website built five years earlier, and stationery printed two years prior. They were concerned about the cost and disruption of a full rebrand. They were equally concerned about investing in a refresh that left underlying problems intact.

## What a Brand Refresh Is

A brand refresh updates and modernises a brand's visual expression without changing its fundamental identity. The logo mark may be refined — proportions adjusted, typography updated, spacing improved — but it remains recognisably the same logo. The colour palette may be modernised — a dated mid-tone blue shifted to a cleaner, more contemporary navy — without changing the essential brand colour.

A refresh is appropriate when:
- The core brand identity is sound but the execution is visually dated
- The business has strong brand recognition it doesn't want to risk losing
- The assets (vehicle livery, signage) have significant remaining useful life
- The primary problem is "it looks old" rather than "it represents us incorrectly"

## What a Full Rebrand Is

A full rebrand rebuilds the brand identity from scratch — new name (sometimes), new logo, new colour palette, new typography, new brand voice. It is appropriate when:
- The brand actively misrepresents the business or its positioning
- The business has pivoted significantly and the old brand is associated with a different offer
- The business is entering a new market where the old brand would be a liability
- Brand recognition in the existing market is low or the existing recognition is negative

## What We Found

We started with a brand audit — reviewing all existing assets and asking the business a set of structured questions:

- Do your clients recognise your brand across different touchpoints?
- Does the brand feel consistent with the quality of work you deliver?
- Have you lost work because your brand didn't project the right impression?
- Are there specific client segments where the brand is working against you?

The answers were revealing. The business had strong recognition among existing clients — the established local market knew them. The brand was working against them in one specific context: when quoting for larger commercial contracts, the aged brand was creating a perception of scale and capability that didn't match the business's actual capacity.

The logo itself was a stock mark with a customised wordmark — inexpensive in origin and visually generic. The colour palette was a mid-2000s combination of mid-blue and silver that dated the brand immediately. The typography was inconsistent across different applications.

## The Recommendation

A refresh would have been insufficient. The underlying mark was too generic and too dated to modernise without losing the original intent entirely. The colour palette needed a structural change, not a tweak. The typography system needed establishing from scratch.

But a full rebrand with new name and complete identity overhaul was also unnecessary. The business name was distinctive and established. The client base was loyal.

We recommended what we call a **structured rebrand**: new logo, new colour palette, new typography — but built with continuity in mind. The new identity preserved the category cues (professional, local, established) while modernising the execution and removing the generic quality.

## The Outcome

The business replaced vehicle livery over twelve months as vehicles came in for servicing — avoiding the cost of premature replacement. New stationery was printed immediately. The website was redesigned to the new brand standards. The total investment was managed over two financial years.

Eighteen months after the rebrand, the business reported that several larger commercial clients had specifically referenced the quality and professionalism of their presentation in initial contact — an outcome the old brand had consistently failed to produce.

## The Decision Framework

If you're uncertain whether to refresh or rebrand, start with these questions:

1. Is the problem with the execution (dated style) or the concept (wrong positioning)?
2. How strong is your existing brand recognition, and what is the cost of losing it?
3. What are the practical asset replacement costs, and can they be managed over time?
4. Are you losing work specifically because of how your brand presents you?

If the answers point toward execution problems with strong existing recognition, a refresh. If they point toward concept problems or weak recognition, a rebrand.
