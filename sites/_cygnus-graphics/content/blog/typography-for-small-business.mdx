## Why Typography Gets Neglected

When small businesses put together their first brand, typography is often the last thing they think about. A logo is chosen, colours are picked — sometimes well, sometimes arbitrarily — and then a font gets selected, usually from a limited dropdown in whatever tool was used. Times New Roman, Arial, or whichever Google Font seemed vaguely professional at the time.

This approach produces a brand that looks amateur regardless of how good the logo is. Typography is the typographic equivalent of body language — it communicates professionalism, personality, and quality before anyone has read a word.

## Start with Two Typefaces, Not One

Most professional brand identities use two typefaces: a display or heading typeface for titles, and a body typeface for running text. Two typefaces used consistently creates hierarchy, interest, and professionalism. One typeface everywhere can look flat. Three or more creates chaos.

**The pairing principle:** Contrast works better than similarity. A geometric sans-serif heading typeface pairs well with a humanist serif body typeface. A bold slab serif heading pairs well with a clean, neutral sans-serif body.

**What to avoid:** Pairing two typefaces that are similar but not the same (two sans-serifs with different proportions, for example) creates an uncomfortable visual tension that reads as an error rather than a design decision.

## Serif vs Sans-Serif: When to Use Each

**Serif typefaces** (with the small horizontal strokes at the ends of letterforms — Times, Garamond, Georgia) carry associations of tradition, authority, and establishment. They are commonly used by legal, financial, and heritage businesses. They are excellent for body text in print.

**Sans-serif typefaces** (without serifs — Helvetica, Futura, Gill Sans) carry associations of modernity, clarity, and technology. They dominate digital interfaces. They are excellent for headings and short text elements. Many contemporary brands use sans-serif exclusively for a clean, modern feel.

**Neither is inherently better.** The right choice depends on the brand's personality and sector.

## Type Scale: Establishing Hierarchy

A type scale defines the sizes of headings, subheadings, body text, and captions. Without a defined scale, you end up with arbitrary size choices that lack the visual rhythm that makes design feel ordered and professional.

A simple starting point for most small business print and digital applications:

- **Display/headline:** 36–60pt (print), 36–48px (web)
- **Section heading:** 24–30pt/px
- **Subheading:** 18–20pt/px
- **Body text:** 10–12pt (print), 16–18px (web)
- **Caption/small print:** 8–9pt (print), 13–14px (web)

Stick to the scale. Don't create intermediate sizes just because something "needs to be a bit bigger." The discipline of a consistent scale is what creates visual order.

## Spacing: The Part Everyone Gets Wrong

**Leading (line spacing):** Body text set with too-tight line spacing is fatiguing to read. For body text, set leading at approximately 1.4–1.6× the type size. At 10pt type, that's 14–16pt leading.

**Tracking (letter spacing):** Body text should have minimal or zero tracking. All-caps text — headlines, labels, signage — benefits from increased tracking (5–10% of type size) because capital letters are designed for mixed-case use and sit poorly tight together in all-caps settings.

**Margins and measure:** Body text becomes difficult to read when lines are too long. The optimal line length is 55–75 characters for print, 45–65 for screen. Wide single-column layouts with long lines are a common problem in self-designed brochures.

## Licensing: The Hidden Problem

Many designers and businesses use fonts without checking the licence. Google Fonts are free for commercial use — both web and print. Most other fonts — typefaces you purchase once and install — have licences that specify: personal use only, web only, or print only. A font that is correctly licensed for a printed brochure may not be licensed for web use in the same brand.

When establishing your brand typefaces, confirm the licence covers all the ways you intend to use the font — print, web, signage, merchandise, and video.
