## Understanding Chalk Soil

The South Downs ridge that runs across East Sussex from the Kent border to the coast at Eastbourne sits on chalk. The gardens of Lewes, Seaford, the villages on the Downs escarpment, and much of the coastal strip share the same underlying geology — thin, alkaline, free-draining soil that warms quickly in spring but dries out rapidly in summer.

Chalk soil has a high pH — typically 7.5–8.5. This alkalinity limits the availability of certain nutrients, particularly iron, manganese, and boron. Acid-loving plants (rhododendrons, azaleas, camellias, pieris) that require a low pH simply cannot access the iron they need and become chlorotic (yellowing leaves) and fail.

The approach that works is not to try to acidify the soil — this is difficult and temporary — but to choose plants adapted to alkaline conditions. There are hundreds of excellent plants in this category. A chalk garden can be as beautiful and productive as any other.

## Trees for Chalk Gardens

**Field maple (Acer campestre)** — native, wildlife-value, good autumn colour. An excellent choice for smaller gardens and hedging.

**Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus)** — tolerates chalk well. Useful for pleaching and formal hedging where beech (which also suits chalk) might struggle in wetter sites.

**Beech (Fagus sylvatica)** — the classic South Downs hedgerow tree. Responds well to pruning, holds its leaves through winter in hedge form. Best on well-drained chalk.

**Whitebeam (Sorbus aria)** — native to chalk downland. Silver underside to the leaves is distinctive. Excellent wildlife value, berries for birds.

**Cherry (Prunus species)** — ornamental cherries and wild cherry (Prunus avium) tolerate chalk well. Wild cherry on chalk produces good autumn colour.

## Shrubs for Chalk Soil

**Buddleja (Buddleja davidii)** — loves chalk. Prolifically self-seeds on chalk banks. Hard-prune in spring to produce the best summer flowering.

**Viburnum** — most species tolerate alkaline conditions. Viburnum tinus flowers through winter; Viburnum opulus (guelder rose) is native to chalk scrub and excellent for wildlife.

**Box (Buxus sempervirens)** — classically associated with chalk downland. Used for formal hedging, topiary, and structure planting. (Note box blight pressure in recent years — source from reputable nurseries.)

**Lavender (Lavandula angustifolia)** — thrives in the free-draining, alkaline conditions of South Downs chalk. Essential for the dry, sunny chalk garden.

**Rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus)** — as with lavender, Mediterranean herbs prefer the sharp drainage and alkalinity of chalk soil.

**Cistus (Rock rose)** — drought-tolerant, chalk-tolerant, and produces a prolific display of papery flowers in early summer. Not hardy in severe winters but usually reliable in coastal East Sussex.

**Forsythia** — reliable early-spring flowering shrub on chalk. Often overlooked as too commonplace, but on chalk sites where choice is limited it earns its place.

## Perennials and Herbaceous Plants

**Salvia (Ornamental sage)** — many salvias thrive in alkaline, free-draining conditions. Salvia 'Caradonna', Salvia nemorosa, and the more tender Salvia guarantica all perform well.

**Scabiosa (Scabious)** — native to chalk grassland. Field scabious and small scabious are wildflower natives that look superb in informal planting schemes on chalk soils.

**Knautia macedonica** — related to scabious, excellent on chalk, long flowering period from June to September, rich crimson-red flowers.

**Achillea (Yarrow)** — tolerates poor, alkaline, free-draining soil extremely well. Flowers from June to September. Available in creams, yellows, and pinks.

**Verbascum (Mullein)** — tall architectural spires that self-seed readily on chalk. Biennial, but self-perpetuating in the right conditions.

**Clematis** — many clematis species are native to alkaline soils. Clematis viticella and its cultivars are the most reliable and chalk-tolerant. Clematis montana is vigorous and reliable.

## Lawn on Chalk Soil

Chalk lawns dry out quickly in summer. They benefit from:
- A lawn seed mix that includes fine fescues — more drought-tolerant than ryegrass-dominated mixes
- Deep but infrequent watering rather than light frequent irrigation
- Resistance to the temptation to cut too short — longer grass (40–50mm in dry periods) shades the root zone and retains moisture

## Working With the Soil, Not Against It

The most successful chalk gardens embrace the conditions rather than fighting them. With the right plant selection, chalk soil produces gardens of real character — the wildflower-rich aesthetic of chalk grassland, the Mediterranean abundance of lavenders and salvias, the structure of beech and box — that are genuinely difficult to achieve on heavier, wetter soils.
