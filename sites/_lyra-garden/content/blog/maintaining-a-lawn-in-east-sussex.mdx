## East Sussex Lawn Conditions

East Sussex has a relatively mild maritime climate, but the variation within the county is significant. Coastal lawns around Eastbourne, Seaford, and Hove experience mild winters, moderate summers, and relatively consistent rainfall. Inland gardens around Uckfield, Hailsham, and the Weald have cooler winters, hotter summers, and more drought stress.

Chalk-based soils on the South Downs are free-draining and drought-susceptible. Clay soils in the Weald hold water and can become waterlogged in autumn and winter. Understanding your soil type is the starting point for a sensible lawn care programme.

## Spring: March–May

**March — First cuts and renovation:**
As soil temperatures rise above 7°C, growth begins. The first cut should be high — 50–60mm. Scalping a lawn that has been dormant causes more harm than benefit.

If the lawn has thatch (a spongy layer of dead grass and organic matter at the surface), spring is the time to scarify — either by hand with a spring-tine rake or with a mechanical scarifier. Follow scarification with aeration (spiking with a garden fork or hollow-tine aerator) to improve drainage and root zone health. Overseed bare patches with a lawn seed matched to your conditions (shade-tolerant mix for shaded areas, fine fescue mix for well-drained chalk sites).

**April–May — Feed and build:**
Apply a spring/summer lawn fertiliser with an NPK analysis weighted toward nitrogen (e.g., 15-5-10 or similar). Nitrogen drives the leaf growth that fills in the lawn and builds the dense sward that outcompetes weeds. Apply evenly with a lawn spreader and water in if rain doesn't fall within 24 hours.

Gradually lower the mowing height as the lawn establishes — most lawns suit a height of 30–40mm for the main growing season.

**Weed control:** Broadleaf weeds (dandelions, plantains, clover, thistles) can be treated with selective lawn weedkiller in spring once the grass is actively growing. Treatment is more effective in warm soil.

## Summer: June–August

**Mowing frequency:** During vigorous growth, a weekly mow maintains height and encourages tillering (the production of side shoots that create a denser sward). In a dry spell, reduce mowing frequency and raise the cutting height — this reduces stress on the grass.

**Drought management:** East Sussex summers can produce extended dry periods. A drought-stressed lawn goes brown and dormant — this is a normal survival response, not death. Brown lawns usually recover when rain returns. Unless the lawn is ornamental or for a specific purpose, restricting watering is usually the right approach.

If you water, water deeply and infrequently — 20–25mm per watering, once or twice a week — rather than light daily irrigation. Light irrigation encourages shallow rooting; deep irrigation encourages roots to track moisture downward, building drought resilience.

**Avoid feeding in drought:** Do not apply fertiliser to a drought-stressed lawn. It will cause scorch and do no good.

## Autumn: September–November

**September — Renovation month:**
Autumn is the most important season for lawn renovation. Soil is still warm, moisture returns, and the grass has the rest of the autumn growing season to recover from renovation work.

Scarify and aerate as in spring — but more deeply if the thatch layer is significant. Hollow-tine aeration in autumn removes cores and creates channels that improve winter drainage, reducing the waterlogging and moss pressure that East Sussex clay-soil lawns are prone to.

**Overseed and topdress:**
Overseed bare and thin areas with autumn-mix seed (typically more ryegrass for faster establishment in cooling conditions). Topdress with a sandy loam or purpose-made lawn topdressing — work it into the aeration holes with a stiff brush. Topdressing improves drainage, levels the surface, and provides a seedbed for overseeding.

**Autumn feed:**
Switch to an autumn lawn fertiliser — lower nitrogen, higher potassium and phosphorus (e.g., 6-5-10). Potassium hardens the grass for winter; high nitrogen in autumn produces soft, disease-susceptible growth.

**Moss:**
Moss is a symptom of poor conditions — shade, compaction, poor drainage, low pH, or excessive thatch. Applying lawn sand (ferrous sulphate) kills moss; but unless the underlying conditions are addressed, moss returns. Aeration and improved drainage are the structural fixes for moss problems.

**October–November:**
Keep mowing until growth stops — usually late October or November in East Sussex. Keep the lawn clear of fallen leaves; a layer of leaves deprives the grass of light and promotes disease.

## Winter: December–February

**Minimal intervention:**
Avoid walking on frosted or waterlogged lawn. The grass crowns are vulnerable to frost damage when compressed. Compaction in wet winter conditions is the most common cause of lawn deterioration.

**Drainage issues:**
Areas that hold surface water through winter need structural improvement — hollow-tine aeration in autumn, or in severe cases, a French drain. Surface drainage problems don't improve on their own.
