## The East Sussex Spring Window

East Sussex has a relatively mild maritime climate, but late frosts are common into April — particularly inland around Uckfield and the Weald. Coastal gardens around Eastbourne and Seaford often see warmer springs than inland sites. This variability means spring preparation needs to account for where you are, not just what month it is.

February is early. The soil is often still cold and waterlogged from winter rainfall. Working on heavy soil when it's wet causes compaction that can take a whole growing season to recover from. Test the soil — if it compacts into a ball and retains its shape, it's too wet to work. If it crumbles, you're good.

## Lawn Care: First Priorities

**February:** Avoid walking on the lawn if it's waterlogged or frosted. Compaction and damage are hard to reverse. If the lawn needs drainage work, identify the problem areas for attention in March.

**Early March:** Once soil temperatures begin to rise and the lawn shows signs of growth, the first light mow can happen — set the mower blade high (50–60mm) and don't take more than one third of the growth off. The first cut is about tidying, not shortening.

**Mid March onwards:** Begin the spring renovation:
- **Scarify** to remove the thatch layer (dead grass and debris) that has accumulated over winter
- **Aerate** compacted areas with a garden fork or hollow-tine aerator — this improves drainage and air movement to the roots
- **Overseed** bare patches with a lawn seed appropriate to your shade/sun conditions
- **Feed** with a spring lawn fertiliser once the soil temperature is consistently above 7°C

## Borders and Beds

**Cut back dead growth from last year.** Ornamental grasses, perennial seed heads, and any frost-damaged material should be cleared. Many gardeners leave some of this through winter for wildlife value — February is the right time to cut it back.

**Divide and refresh perennials.** Clump-forming perennials (hostas, geraniums, astilbes) benefit from lifting and dividing every 2–3 years. Early spring — as new growth is just starting — is the best time. Replant the vigorous outer sections, discard the exhausted centre.

**Prepare soil for planting.** Add well-rotted compost or manure to beds and borders. East Sussex soils vary — chalk on the South Downs, clay in the Weald, sandy loam in coastal areas. Each benefits from organic matter, but the way it works differs. Sandy soils need more organic matter for moisture retention; clay soils benefit from grit addition alongside organic matter to improve structure.

**Weed early.** Annual weeds are much easier to manage when they're small. Getting on top of them in March — before they set seed — saves work throughout the summer. Perennial weeds (bindweed, couch grass, ground elder) should be dug out root and all while the soil is workable.

## Pruning: What to Do Now

**Roses:** Hybrid tea and floribunda roses should be pruned in early spring — in East Sussex, typically late February to mid March. Cut to outward-facing buds, remove crossing branches, and reduce the overall height by approximately one third to one half. Climbing and rambler roses are pruned after flowering.

**Buddleja (butterfly bush):** Hard prune to approximately 30cm from the ground in March. This produces the most vigorous flowering growth.

**Hydrangeas:** The timing depends on the type. Mophead and lacecap hydrangeas (H. macrophylla) should have last year's dead flower heads removed now, cutting back to a pair of healthy buds — but avoid cutting into older wood. Panicle and smooth hydrangeas can be cut back harder.

**Fruit trees:** Late winter is the time for dormant pruning of apples and pears. Remove crossing branches, dead wood, and any that are creating a congested centre. Open up the crown to improve light and air circulation.

## What to Plant in March

March is reliable for:
- Hardy annuals sown directly into prepared ground (calendula, cornflower, nasturtium)
- Onion sets and shallots
- Early potatoes (once the risk of severe frost has passed — usually late March in most East Sussex locations)
- Bare-root roses and trees planted from November to March
- Plug plants of tender perennials can be ordered for delivery from late March onwards (harden off before planting out)
