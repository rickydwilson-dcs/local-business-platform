## 3. The positioning — SHARPENED

Ricky's own words, and the clearest statement of the pitch yet:

> We may be small but we can create incredible sites that compete with London/NYC agencies
> for a fraction of the cost and a tiny fraction of the client effort.

That is a three-part claim, and all three parts should land:

1. **The work competes with London and New York agencies.** Not "good for a small studio" —
   genuinely comparable. Ambition is the point.
2. **A fraction of the cost.**
3. **A tiny fraction of the client's effort.**

**The site is the proof of the first claim.** This is why the design ambition matters and why
it is not vanity: a page that genuinely looks like it came out of a London studio _is_ the
argument. Nothing else on the page has to work as hard. Conversely, an ordinary-looking page
makes the claim unsupportable no matter how well it is written.

**One honesty constraint.** Do not invent a competitor's prices to dramatise the comparison —
no "London agencies charge £30,000 for this". We have no sourced figure, and a fabricated
one is a false claim on a real business's site. Make the comparison qualitatively (the
standard of the work, the calibre of studio) and let the real DCS prices speak for
themselves. If a direction wants a number, the only honest numbers on the page are DCS's own.

**Also avoid the small-and-proud trap.** "We may be small" is context for Ricky, not a line
for the page. Never open with the limitation. The page should read as though the studio is
simply this good; the smallness is what makes it _personal and cheap_, not what excuses the
work.

**The expanded version:**

Most studios expect you to arrive with a brief, copy, images and a clear idea of what you
want. DCS doesn't. It asks the right questions, writes the content, makes the design
decisions, builds it, hosts it, and keeps it running.

The differentiators, strongest first:

1. **You're hiring a person, not a company.** Nothing outsourced, nothing offshored.
2. **Agency-quality output** — considered design, well-written copy, fast and secure builds.
3. **Low effort for the client** — everything drawn out through conversation, not a brief.
4. **Content handled.** Clients don't need to know what to say.
5. **No technical knowledge required** — no CMS, no login, no dashboard to learn.
6. **All-in pricing** — setup plus a low monthly covering hosting, security, updates,
   support.
7. **An ongoing relationship** — a managed service, not a hand-off.

## 4. Tone of voice

Plain-spoken and confident. Warm but composed. Personal — "I" is often better than "we".
Reassuring without being soft. Honest about what things cost.

**Elevated, not matey.** v1 briefs pushed some directions into a blokey, on-site register.
Aim closer to a good architecture or brand studio: assured, quiet, precise. The reader
should feel they are dealing with someone whose taste they can borrow.

**Banned:** "synergy", "bespoke solutions", "leveraging", "transform your business", "in
today's digital landscape", "just a small operation", "nothing fancy". Also avoid trade
slang used as decoration — "graft", "the tools", "on the job" — unless a specific line
genuinely earns it.

**Never mention the tech stack.** No Next.js, no Tailwind, no Vercel, no "platform".

## 12. Visual direction — READ THIS, IT IS THE BIG CHANGE

The target is **an elevated design studio**. The site should look like it was made by
someone whose taste you would pay for.

**Explicitly ruled out:**

- Hi-vis yellow and black, hazard tape, chevrons, stencil type, "under construction"
  signalling of any kind.
- Site-notice, job-sheet, docket, work-order, scaffold-board and road-sign metaphors. Round
  1 produced six of these; they are not wanted.
- Any treatment that reads as a building site, a merchant's counter, or industrial safety
  signage.
- Generic template energy at the other extreme: centred hero, three equal icon cards,
  purple-to-blue gradient on white.

**What to aim at instead:** restraint, generous space, real typographic craft, a confident
and unusual colour position, considered motion, and detail that rewards a second look.
Think brand studio, architecture practice, quiet luxury, editorial. The work itself and the
typography should carry the page.

Colour is wide open, including bold and saturated — the objection is to _industrial safety_
palettes and construction signifiers, not to colour or to boldness. An elevated page can
absolutely be dark, or vivid, or dense. It just cannot look like a hoarding.
