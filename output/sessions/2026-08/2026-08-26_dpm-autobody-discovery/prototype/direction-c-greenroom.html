The journal

Written by the man who did it

Every entry here is signed. Nobody writes about a job they did not stand in front of. It runs behind real life, so some months are thin.

21 August 2026 / Stage 09 — Out in the daylight

She went home on Tuesday, in the rain

D Dave — paint and finish

The Sprite went back to Kent on Tuesday morning, on the back of a trailer, in the rain. Nine months and two weeks with us. I would have liked it to leave in the sun, but you take the day you get.

I walked round it twice before it went up the ramps. That is not for the owner's benefit, it is for mine — once it has gone I cannot do anything about what I have missed. There is a spot low on the nearside rear quarter, behind the wheel, where the reflection breaks about a millimetre earlier than it does on the other side. Nobody will ever see it. I will know it is there for years.

He rang on Thursday to say he had had it out on the lanes above Alfriston. That is the part of the job that pays for the rest of it.

7 August 2026 / Stage 08 — Clear, cut and polish

The last two thousandths of an inch

P Paul — colour, mixing, polish

Cutting back is the stage everybody thinks is polishing. It is not. Polishing is what you do at the end to a surface that is already flat. Cutting is taking the top off the clear until it is flat, and there is not very much of it to take.

Four coats of clear went on this one. I will take maybe two thousandths of an inch back off with a 1500 and then a 3000, and that still leaves plenty. Take too much and you are through to the base, the panel goes back in the booth, and you have cost everybody a fortnight. So you go slowly and you keep the pad moving.

The machine does the work. The skill is knowing when to stop, and the honest answer is that you feel it through the pad before you see it in the panel.

19 July 2026 / Stage 06 — Primer, and the guide coat

The guide coat found what my eye did not

E Ellis — metalwork and prep

A guide coat is a mist of black over grey primer. You flat it back with a block and wherever the black stays, the panel is low. It is the cheapest tool in the shop and it is the only one that has never once flattered me.

The offside front wing came up clean, then held a black line about six inches long running with the crown of the arch. I went again with a finer grade and it was still there. So it was not the primer. It was the metal underneath.

What was under it was a repair somebody had done properly — properly for 1974 — in lead, and a fair amount of it, and the lead had moved. That is three weeks we had not planned for. Back to bare metal, the lead out, a small let-in piece welded and planished, and then the primer started again from the beginning.

I would rather find it there than watch it come up in the clear coat six weeks later. I am not going to pretend it was a good Tuesday.

28 June 2026 / Stage 07 — Colour, decided in daylight

Two greens, and neither of them was hers

P Paul — colour, mixing, polish

The plate on the bulkhead said one green. The car, wherever we could still find original paint on it, was a different one. Both are correct for the year, which is no help at all.

We sprayed out cards of each and held them against the bare wing in the doorway, because the light in the booth will lie to you and daylight will not. Under the booth lamps the factory code read almost blue. Outside it was fine. That difference is the whole reason this bit gets done standing in the yard with the door open.

In the end we used neither. There was a strip of untouched original under the boot seal, about the width of a finger, and we matched to that. It is a shade warmer than the code. It is what left the factory on this car, which turns out to be a different question from what left the factory that year.

The nine stages

What a car goes through here

These are our names for them, not the trade's. The shortest car we have finished took seven months. The one in the far bay has been here thirty-one months and is not finished yet.

  1. 01 In, and photographed as she came Every panel, every gap, every bit of rot, before a spanner touches it. 1 day
  2. 02 Off it comes Strip to a shell. Bagged, labelled, shelved. Nothing goes in a box unmarked. 1–2 weeks
  3. 03 Down to what is really there Media blast or chemical strip, then the conversation nobody enjoys. 2–3 weeks
  4. 04 Metal first Panels made, let in, welded, planished. This is where the time goes. 3 weeks – 5 months
  5. 05 Straight before shiny Block work over bare metal. If it needs filler to look right, it is not right. 3–6 weeks
  6. 06 Primer, and the guide coat Etch, epoxy, high-build. Then black mist and a long block, twice. 2–4 weeks
  7. 07 Colour, decided in daylight Spray-out cards judged outside the booth, against the car's own metal. 2 days
  8. 08 Clear, cut and polish Four coats, a week to harden, then back to flat by hand and machine. 2–3 weeks
  9. 09 Out in the daylight Built back up, gaps set, then pushed outside and walked round twice. 2–4 weeks

Materials index

What goes on, in the order it goes on

Swatches are indicative, mixed for this page rather than sprayed out. Period colour names are the manufacturers' own.

  • Etch primer

    Goes straight onto clean bare steel. Thin, and it bites.

  • Two-pack epoxy primer

    The barrier coat. Everything above it depends on this one being right.

  • High-build primer

    Sprayed to be taken off again. What is left is a flat panel.

  • Guide coat, black

    A mist you cut through. The stage-06 entry is about this.

  • Lead loading

    Tinman's solder, wiped with a wooden paddle and tallow. Period-correct.

  • Old English White

    Warmer than anybody expects it to be, and wrong in a modern white.

  • Almond Green

    Reads grey in the booth and green in the yard. Judge it outside.

  • British Racing Green

    Not one colour. There are a dozen and the owner always has a view.

  • Opalescent Silver Blue

    Early metallic. Blotches if the gun is wrong, so the gun is not wrong.

  • Signal Red

    The hardest colour in the shop to lay flat and the fastest to fade.

  • Two-pack clear

    Colourless out of the gun. Everything you read as depth is this.

  • Compound and finishing polish

    Coarse then fine then nothing. Stopping is the skilled part.

Tool portrait — number one

The gun I keep for the small stuff

Suction feed, older than anybody in the building, and the brass ring on the air cap is polished because of hands rather than because anybody polished it. It came out of a shop in Eastbourne that shut in the eighties.

We have newer guns. They atomise better and they waste far less material. This one gets used on the small work — a filler surround, the inside of a door shut, anywhere the pattern needs to be tight and I want to feel the trigger rather than trust it.

You can hear when it is right. There is a note the air makes when the fluid and the fan are balanced, and when it is wrong it spits and tells you before the panel does.

It gets stripped and the passages cleaned every time it is used, which is about the only thing in this workshop that has never once been left until the morning.

— Dave
