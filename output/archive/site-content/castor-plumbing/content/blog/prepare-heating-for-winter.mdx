## Why Autumn is the Right Time

East Sussex sits in a relatively mild coastal climate, but winter cold snaps — particularly the inland areas around Uckfield and the Weald — can be sharp. Boiler breakdowns spike in November and December as heating systems that have sat idle through summer are switched back on, and engineers are at their busiest.

Preparing your heating system in September or October means any faults are found and repaired before the cold arrives, and before the wait for a plumber stretches to several days.

## Step 1: Switch the Heating On

The most basic check. Switch your heating on and let it run for 20–30 minutes. Listen for unusual noises — banging, gurgling, or persistent ticking that doesn't settle. Feel each radiator to confirm it heats up fully. A cold spot at the top of a radiator usually means trapped air; a cold radiator entirely may mean a stuck or failed thermostatic radiator valve (TRV).

## Step 2: Check Boiler Pressure

Combi boilers should operate with a pressure gauge reading between 1 and 1.5 bar when cold. If the needle is below 1 bar, the system needs repressurising. Your boiler manual will have instructions for doing this yourself using the filling loop — or call us if you're not comfortable.

If pressure drops again within a few weeks of repressurising, there is a leak somewhere in the system. That needs investigating before winter.

## Step 3: Bleed the Radiators

Air accumulates in radiators over summer. Bleeding removes air and allows full water flow. You'll need a radiator bleed key (available from any DIY shop).

With the heating off and radiators cold:
1. Hold a cloth under the bleed valve at the top corner of the radiator
2. Turn the bleed key anti-clockwise slowly — you'll hear a hiss as air escapes
3. When water starts to trickle out, tighten the valve
4. Check the boiler pressure after bleeding all radiators — bleeding drops system pressure

## Step 4: Check Radiator Valves

Each radiator has two valves — a lockshield valve on one side and either a manual or thermostatic valve on the other. TRVs can seize if they haven't moved all summer.

Turn each TRV to a higher number and back. If a TRV feels stiff or stuck, the pin inside may have seized. A stuck TRV pin will leave the radiator either permanently off or permanently on. This is a straightforward repair — replacing or freeing the TRV pin.

## Step 5: Book an Annual Service

An annual boiler service by a Gas Safe registered engineer checks combustion efficiency, heat exchanger condition, flue integrity, gas pressure, safety devices, and controls. It's the difference between a boiler that surprises you with a December breakdown and one that runs reliably through winter.

Services should ideally happen every 12 months. If your boiler hasn't been serviced in two or more years, prioritise this. At DCS Plumbing, we recommend booking in September — availability is much better than in October and November.

## Step 6: Lagging Exposed Pipes

Pipes in unheated spaces — lofts, garages, under suspended floors — are vulnerable to freezing in a cold snap. Foam pipe lagging is inexpensive and straightforward to fit. The outside pipework from the boiler flue and the condensate drain pipe are particularly important — a frozen condensate pipe is one of the most common causes of boiler lockout in January.

## Step 7: Test the Thermostat and Timer

Set your thermostat and programmer now, before you need them. Check the programmer is set to the right day and time (they often reset after a power cut). A thermostat that isn't working correctly means the boiler may run continuously or not at all — both waste energy and the latter means a cold house.

If your thermostat is more than 10 years old, a smart thermostat replacement is worth considering. Nest and Hive thermostats pay back their cost in reduced gas bills within a couple of years for most households.

## Common Autumn Callouts We Receive

In September and October, the most common calls we receive are:

- Boilers that won't fire after summer shutdown (often a lockout that resets, but sometimes a faulty component)
- Cold radiators caused by seized TRVs
- System pressure loss from a filling loop left partially open or a developing leak
- Noisy systems caused by sludge and scale buildup (addressable with a power flush)
