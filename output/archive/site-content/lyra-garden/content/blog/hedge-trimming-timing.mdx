## Why Timing Matters

Hedge trimming timing matters for two distinct reasons: the health and appearance of the hedge, and the wildlife it supports.

In the UK, cutting or destroying a bird's nest while it is in use or being built is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Hedges are the nesting habitat of choice for many garden birds — sparrows, blackbirds, robins, dunnocks, and finches all commonly nest in hedges. The nesting season runs approximately from March to August, with peak activity from April to July.

This doesn't mean hedges can't be touched during this period — it means you must check for active nests before cutting.

## The Nesting Season Rule

**Before any hedge cut from March to August:** Check for active nests first. Walk slowly along the hedge line; watch for birds flying in and out. Part the foliage gently and look for nests. If you find an active nest (eggs or young birds present), stop — that section of hedge cannot be cut until the nest is abandoned, typically 4–6 weeks after the eggs are laid.

Most professional gardeners will not cut hedges without a nest check during the nesting season, and will stop work immediately if a nest is found.

## Best Times to Trim by Hedge Type

### Formal Clipped Hedges (Box, Yew, Privet, Lonicera)

These are typically cut once or twice a year to maintain a tight, formal shape.

**Primary cut:** Late August to September. By this time, the nesting season is largely over, the summer growth has extended fully, and a cut now produces a neat finish that holds through winter. The hedge has time to harden off slightly before cold weather.

**Secondary cut (if needed):** May–June (first half of June at the latest). This tidies up spring growth before summer nesting activity peaks. Always check for nests before cutting.

**Avoid:** Cutting formal hedges in late spring (April–May) unless it's a brief tidy rather than a significant cut — late spring cuts stimulate another flush of growth that immediately needs cutting again.

### Box Hedging

Box has two trimming windows: late May (after the first growth flush) and late August to September. Box blight — a serious fungal disease now widespread in East Sussex — spreads more rapidly in warm, damp conditions. Trimming on a dry day and ensuring good air circulation around the hedge reduces blight risk. Clean your shears with a diluted disinfectant between hedges.

### Beech and Hornbeam

Cut once a year in late August. Beech and hornbeam hedges retain their leaves through winter (marcescent leaves) — a cut in late August produces a neat appearance that holds through winter. A second cut is rarely needed.

### Leylandii (Leyland Cypress)

Leylandii can be cut 2–3 times a year: late spring (May), late summer (August), and early autumn (September). Leylandii grows very fast and a single annual cut often isn't enough to maintain size control. Note that leylandii cannot regenerate from old wood — never cut back into brown, leafless wood. Maintain a living green surface at all times.

### Mixed Native Hedges (Hawthorn, Blackthorn, Hazel, Elderberry)

Native hedges are the most ecologically valuable and require the most care around nesting season.

**Best timing:** Late August to late February (outside the nesting season). A cut in August–September after the breeding season and before winter maximises wildlife value. The berries of hawthorn and elderberry are an important late autumn food source — delaying cutting until after October allows these to be taken by birds.

**Avoid:** Cutting native hedges during April, May, June, and July unless a nest check confirms no active nests.

### Laurel

Laurel is vigorous and benefits from a twice-yearly cut — May and August. Use secateurs or a hedge trimmer with large blades; small-bladed trimmers create ragged cuts that brown at the edges. Laurel responds well to renovation (hard cutting back) when overgrown, but only in spring.
